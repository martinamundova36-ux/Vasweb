The Unseen Role of Universities in Social Innovation: What New Research Reveals

Author: Martina Mundová

Universities are frequently viewed as vital hubs of knowledge, yet their lasting impact on social enterprises beyond the start-up phase remains ambiguous. According to a new study published in the Social Enterprise Journal, the dynamics of this collaboration shift dramatically as ventures mature.

Introduction

Social innovations and entrepreneurship resonate strongly in modern society, directing natural attention toward academic environments. Universities hold immense potential to educate, connect, and provide foundational support for novel ideas aimed at societal betterment.

Despite this, empirical evidence regarding how social enterprise founders themselves perceive the presence and real support of academic institutions has been scarce. Findings from a fresh study offer valuable insight into this dynamic, moving beyond common theoretical assumptions.

What the study examined

According to the abstract, the research by authors Julia Berg-Postweiler and Carmen Leicht-Scholten investigated how social entrepreneurs experience the role of universities within the social innovation ecosystem. The primary objective was to explore interactions between ventures and academia both during the initial venture creation phase and through ongoing collaboration.

In terms of methodology, the authors utilized primary data by conducting fifteen interviews with German social entrepreneurs, which were subsequently analyzed using thematic analysis to uncover recurring patterns and perspectives.

Findings and interpretation

The findings published in the study indicate that universities can play a significant role within the social innovation ecosystem, proving especially influential during the founding phase. This impact is noted as particularly strong for student entrepreneurs who directly leverage academic surroundings.

On the other hand, according to the abstract, universities are often perceived as less active in later development stages of a social enterprise. Nevertheless, many social entrepreneurs express a strong interest in sustained collaboration while encountering barriers such as limited knowledge and a lack of specific contact points.

Methodology and limitations

The study's design relied on exploring perspectives through the aforementioned fifteen interviews with participants situated exclusively in Germany. Because this is a qualitative investigation tailored to a specific national setting, the findings cannot be automatically generalized to different cultural or institutional contexts without further study.

The authors of this editorial piece also emphasize an important limitation: the full text of the study was not independently verified. Readers should note that all outlined conclusions derive strictly from the publisher-supplied abstract available under DOI: 10.1108/sej-12-2025-0296.

WEBU practical proposal

The study's conclusions open the door to editorial recommendations that small business owners, web designers, and managers can readily implement. If you operate a small business or online store, building partnerships with local educational institutions via a dedicated website subpage labeled 'Academic Collaboration' can eliminate the barrier of missing contact points.

In terms of marketing and web design, we recommend establishing a clearly structured section outlining student internships, research projects, and knowledge-sharing opportunities. In workplace management, this translates to implementing internal mentoring programs for young talent, actively bridging the gap between theory and practice that scaling social ventures often miss.

Conclusion

The role of universities in the social innovation ecosystem undergoes a remarkable transformation according to the research. While they act as visible and inspiring actors during early phases, they frequently transition into silent partners later on, making sustained collaboration challenging.

This evolution from a visible actor to a silent one warrants renewed attention from university leadership, educators, policymakers, and practitioners alike. The future success of social innovations will depend heavily on our collective ability to remove communication barriers and construct lasting bridges between academia and enterprise.
