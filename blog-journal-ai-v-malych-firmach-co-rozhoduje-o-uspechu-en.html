AI in Small Businesses: Why Buying a Tool Is Not Enough

Author: Martina Mundová

Artificial intelligence can draft material, sort information and support decisions. But trying an application and producing real business value are very different things. A May 2026 review in Discover Artificial Intelligence synthesized 68 research articles about AI adoption in small and medium-sized enterprises. Its central message is relevant to entrepreneurs: technology should be evaluated together with skills, business processes and measurable objectives.

The research question

The authors examined more than how many firms have started using AI. They investigated the conditions associated with adoption and the constructs researchers use to assess organizational performance. This distinction matters because installing an application is not an outcome. Better decisions, lower error rates, faster service and more effective use of resources are different outcomes and each requires its own evidence and measures.

How the review was conducted

The systematic review included 68 peer-reviewed papers selected from Scopus. Two conceptual frameworks guided interpretation: Technology–Organization–Environment and Diffusion of Innovations. The authors considered technology characteristics, internal organizational conditions and outside pressures. This was a synthesis of published studies rather than an experiment installing the same product in 68 companies. It therefore cannot provide a universal return-on-investment percentage for a specific subscription.

What the findings suggest

Across the research literature, AI adoption was associated with efficiency, decision-making and competitiveness. However, readiness, trust, perceived usefulness and strategic alignment repeatedly appeared as important conditions. Limited resources, technological complexity and resistance to change were recurring barriers. The implications are more nuanced than simply recommending more automation: the value of a tool depends on the quality of the underlying data, employee capabilities and surrounding operating procedures.

Why this matters to entrepreneurs

Smaller firms may make decisions quickly, but they often lack dedicated teams for data governance, cybersecurity and implementation. A tool should therefore be judged against a defined operational difficulty. If customer records are incomplete or responsibilities are unclear, automation may simply produce errors more quickly. Organizational readiness helps distinguish a useful improvement from a fashionable experiment that consumes time without solving the original problem.

An approach worth testing

Start with one repeated process and record its current time requirements, costs and correction rate. Decide what success would mean, who reviews the output and which sensitive data must never be submitted. Run a limited comparison with the existing workflow before making a larger purchase. This is an editorial application of change-management principles, not a universal intervention established across all 68 articles.

What the review does not prove

The review relied on one database and a search period ending in mid-2024. It therefore cannot capture every study or the newest AI tools. Research using different methods and measures cannot automatically be combined into a single causal impact. The findings do not justify guaranteeing increased revenue or predicting job reductions in any individual business without observing its own outcomes.

Conclusion

The useful lesson is not that an entrepreneur needs the largest possible collection of AI applications. It is that technology, people, data and commercial goals must fit together. The research supports a measured approach: understand the problem, test whether the tool improves the relevant result and expand only when the evidence warrants the investment.
