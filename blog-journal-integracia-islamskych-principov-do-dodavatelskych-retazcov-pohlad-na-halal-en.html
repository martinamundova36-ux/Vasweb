Integrating Islamic principles into business theories: A focus on halal meat supply chains

Author: Martina Mundová

This study analyzes the integration of Islamic principles into supply chain management theories. Focusing on New Zealand's export-oriented halal red-meat sector, the research explores the intersection of religious legitimacy, socio-technical systems, and institutional theory. For entrepreneurs in international trade, it provides insights into how religiously-informed accountability shapes operational management and risk profiles within specific market segments.

Introduction

In contemporary global trade, supply chain management faces increasingly complex demands for legitimacy. According to the abstract, this study focuses on how Islamic principles extend existing theories of institutional and socio-technical management. The objective is to understand how religious agency and specific Shariah requirements influence the operations of upstream supply chains in the halal meat sector.

The research centers on the New Zealand export market, which offers an interesting case for examining the clash between global business standards and religious norms. According to the abstract, the authors analyze how these norms are integrated into operational processes. This analysis is crucial for understanding how businesses in diverse cultural contexts can effectively manage stakeholder expectations while maintaining the religious integrity of their products.

What the study examined

The study, according to the abstract, focused on the export-oriented red-meat supply chain in New Zealand. Researchers examined how Shariah requirements define extensions of institutional theory and socio-technical systems theory. The primary focus was on religious legitimacy and the role of human religious agency within these complex systems. The goal was to develop theoretical lenses that better reflect the specific needs of halal certification in the upstream portion of the supply chain.

The research involved 16 stakeholders, including certification bodies, regulators, processors, logistics staff, and slaughterhouse workers. Additionally, an expert survey with six participants was conducted to rate 30 different risk issues. According to the abstract, the authors used reflexive thematic analysis and literature synthesis to interpret the data, aiming for a deeper understanding of the interactions between operational and governance risks in this specific industry.

Findings and interpretation

According to the abstract, the research synthesized seven risk domains and 34 sub-themes, presented through a Halal Certification Risk Cascade. These qualitative domains provide a conceptual view of how operational and governance risks interact within the supply chain. The authors emphasize that these findings represent an interpretation of cross-market legitimacy and religiously accountable task allocation, extending established theoretical frameworks rather than proposing entirely new foundations.

It is important to note that, according to the abstract, the expert ratings in the study do not establish sector-wide consensus or causal relationships. The findings serve more as a conceptual foundation that requires further comparative and operational testing. The significance of the study lies in its ability to offer a structured view of risks specific to halal markets, allowing businesses to better identify and manage potential supply chain issues that could threaten their religious legitimacy.

Methodology and limitations

The methodology of the study, according to the abstract, combines qualitative semi-structured interviews with 16 key actors from the New Zealand halal meat sector and an expert survey with six participants. The authors used reflexive thematic analysis to process the data. This approach allows for a deep understanding of subjective experiences, but its limitations are evident in the small sample size, which may not represent the broader global halal production industry.

According to the abstract, it is necessary to consider that the expert ratings of 30 risk areas are exploratory and do not prove causality. The study focuses on the specific context of New Zealand, which limits the generalizability of the results to other geographical or cultural environments. Without access to the full text, it is impossible to assess whether all external variables that could influence risk profiles in other countries with different regulatory frameworks or religious interpretations were considered.

Business relevance and limitations

Entrepreneurs operating in the halal sector can draw inspiration from the research for systematizing risk management. According to the abstract, it is crucial to view religious legitimacy as a dynamic element that requires constant attention. For example, if an entrepreneur plans to expand into a new market, they could use the identified 34 risk sub-themes to create their own internal supplier audit. This audit should measure compliance with certification requirements at every step of the chain, while accounting for local specifics that the study may not have captured in the New Zealand context.

An important lesson is that risk management in a halal supply chain is not just a technical matter, but a question of religious accountability. Entrepreneurs should consider how their internal processes communicate this accountability to stakeholders. Although the study does not provide a universal guide, its conceptual breakdown of risks can serve as a practical checklist for managers who need to ensure that their operational procedures align with the expectations of religiously sensitive consumers.

Conclusion

The study makes a valuable contribution to understanding the complexity of halal supply chains through the integration of Islamic principles into management theories. According to the abstract, the authors successfully identified key risk domains that can serve as a basis for further research and practical application in the industry. Although the results are limited to the New Zealand context and require further testing, they provide a solid conceptual framework for building legitimacy in international trade.

For future researchers and entrepreneurs, the challenge remains to verify these findings on a broader global scale. According to the abstract, it is essential to continue with comparative studies that would confirm the validity of the identified risks in different cultural and regulatory environments. Overall, the study highlights the need for a holistic approach, where religious accountability becomes an integral part of strategic business management, thereby strengthening consumer trust and the long-term sustainability of business in the global market.
