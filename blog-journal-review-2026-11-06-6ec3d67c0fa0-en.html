Who Benefits from New Work? Employee Perceptions in Family Businesses

The transformation toward new work creates both opportunities and challenges, affecting employees unevenly across roles. This editorial examines recent research published in the Journal of Family Business Management, while carefully considering the limits of available source data.

Introduction

The contemporary workplace is undergoing rapid shifts under the banner of new work. While desk-based workers frequently enjoy remote options and flexible hours, frontline and production employees often experience entirely different operational realities. This divide brings critical questions regarding workplace justice to the forefront of organizational management.

A recent study authored by David Maximilian Gerhard Fischer, Nevena Toporova, and Alwine Mohnen, published in November 2026, explores these dynamics closely. It investigates how family ownership contexts and occupational roles intersect to shape employee evaluations of organizational fairness.

What the study examined

According to the abstract, the study examines how blue-collar and white-collar employees experience and perceive new work practices within family businesses. The primary analytical focus centers on organizational justice within this distinct corporate setting.

The authors conducted a comparative investigation across three German family firms and one non-family firm operating within traditional industries. The research was designed to uncover how occupational roles and organizational structures jointly shape employee responses to flexibility measures.

Findings and interpretation

According to the abstract, the research reveals a notable paradox. Although blue-collar workers face restricted access to flexibility measures, they do not report perceiving distributive injustice, instead attributing these limitations to inherent occupational constraints.

Furthermore, interpersonal justice concerns emerge as a result of reduced decision-maker accessibility during hybrid work arrangements. The findings indicate that while implementation styles vary, core justice mechanisms remain consistent across different organizational contexts.

Methodology and limitations

The study adopted a qualitative multiple-case study design relying on 27 semi-structured interviews conducted across three German family firms and one non-family firm. The resulting data were evaluated using qualitative content analysis.

However, an important limitation must be highlighted: this review relies exclusively on the publisher-supplied abstract without access to the verified full-text article. Consequently, readers should interpret these insights within the boundaries of the summary data, pending complete methodological scrutiny.

WEBU practical proposal

Drawing on these insights, WEBU offers dedicated editorial recommendations for small business owners, web designers, and workplace managers. When deploying digital intranets or operational dashboards, companies must design interfaces that bridge the communication gap between deskless workers and remote staff.

In digital marketing and workplace management, clear communication of company values is essential. If new work policies benefit only a segment of the workforce, internal web portals should integrate dedicated feedback channels to counteract potential interpersonal justice issues and management disconnect.

Conclusion

The research offers valuable perspectives on the boundaries of family ownership and modern work implementation. It suggests that perceived occupational legitimacy dictates fairness judgments rather than mere outcome equality.

For ongoing organizational practice, maintaining open communication channels across all employment tiers remains imperative, ensuring that digital and structural transformations foster collective trust.
