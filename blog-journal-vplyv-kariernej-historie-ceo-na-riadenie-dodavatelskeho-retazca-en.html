The Impact of CEO Job-Hopping on Supply Chain Relationships

Author: Martina Mundová

This study examines how the career history of CEOs influences their strategic focus within supply chain management. According to the abstract, the research suggests that frequent job-hopping among leaders is associated with higher supplier and customer concentration, which may limit the search for new market opportunities and alter how firms approach building long-term business relationships in competitive environments.

Introduction

Supply chain management is not merely a logistical challenge but a reflection of management's strategic priorities. This study investigates how the personal history of CEOs, specifically their experience with job-hopping, shapes the attention they devote to suppliers and customers. According to the abstract, the authors utilize the attention-based view to explain why some firms adopt more conservative approaches when selecting and maintaining key business partners in their operational networks.

The research offers a new perspective on how past career paths influence current corporate decision-making. Instead of focusing solely on transactional factors, the authors analyze how cognitive habits acquired in previous organizations are transferred to the management of critical relationships. According to the abstract, this approach is essential for understanding the variations in purchasing and sales strategies observed across different companies within the Chinese market context.

What the study examined

The primary objective of the study was to examine the relationship between CEO career mobility and the concentration of suppliers and customers. According to the abstract, the authors focused on whether leaders with a history of frequent job-hopping devote less attention to searching for alternative suppliers and expanding into new markets. The research aimed to identify if this attention manifests in measurable activities, such as advertising expenditures or recruitment in procurement and marketing departments.

Furthermore, the study investigated the moderating influence of specific functional experiences, such as R&D or production-line work. According to the abstract, the authors questioned whether these experiences strengthen or weaken the association between career history and the concentration of business partners. The sample included Chinese A-share listed firms from 2012 to 2024, analyzing data regarding the top five trading partners for each company to determine the scope of these relationships.

Findings and interpretation

According to the abstract, firms led by CEOs with more job-hopping experience exhibit higher supplier and customer concentration. These leaders reportedly devote less attention to searching for alternative suppliers, reflected in fewer tender projects and lower procurement-related recruitment. Instead of diversifying their base, they concentrate purchases among established suppliers, which may reduce the firm's flexibility when responding to market shifts or disruptions within the supply chain.

Regarding sales, these CEOs reportedly give lower priority to market expansion, which is reflected in lower advertising expenditure and reduced sales-related recruitment. An interesting finding is that CEO R&D experience strengthens the supplier-side association, whereas production-line experience strengthens the customer-side association. These associations are stronger in non-state-owned firms and those facing performance pressure, while board production-line experience may weaken the customer-side association according to the abstract.

Methodology and limitations

The study's methodology involved analyzing Chinese A-share listed firms from 2012 to 2024. The authors hand-coded CEO job-hopping experience from resumes and measured partner concentration based on disclosed top-five trading partner shares. According to the abstract, fixed-effects models, mechanism and moderation tests, as well as robustness and endogeneity analyses were employed to verify the reliability of the observed associations between the variables and ensure the findings were not driven by simple coincidences.

It is important to note that the study identifies associations rather than causal relationships. According to the abstract, the limitations are defined by the focus on the Chinese market, which may restrict the generalizability of the results to other economic environments. As this is observational research, the influence of unobserved confounding factors cannot be ruled out. Readers should consider that the interpretation of the data depends on the specific context of Chinese firms and their unique governance structures.

Business relevance and limitations

Entrepreneurs and board members should consider how the career history of leadership candidates influences their strategic priorities. According to the abstract, a tendency toward job-hopping may be linked to a more conservative approach to building supply chain relationships. If a firm needs to diversify its supplier base, it may be beneficial to complement the leadership team with individuals who have different experiences and a focus on actively searching for new market opportunities.

For example, if a business owner is planning an expansion into new markets, they should evaluate whether the current CEO is overly focused on an existing network of partners. An entrepreneur could measure the percentage of purchases from new suppliers over the last two years and compare it with the management's career history. However, it must be remembered that this relationship is only a statistical association, and individual leadership capabilities may significantly modify this outcome.

Conclusion

The study provides valuable insight into how past experiences shape managerial attention. According to the abstract, a CEO's career history is not just a resume entry but a cognitive framework that influences how a firm manages its most critical business relationships. For entrepreneurs, this means that when selecting leaders, it is important to consider not only technical skills but also how their past professional journey influences their willingness to take risks when seeking new partners.

The research highlights the need for a balanced composition of management and the board of directors. According to the abstract, various functional experiences, such as R&D or production, can act as moderators that change how a firm treats suppliers and customers. Understanding these dynamics can help firms better align their supply chain strategies and ensure that their leaders devote sufficient attention to innovation and market expansion.
