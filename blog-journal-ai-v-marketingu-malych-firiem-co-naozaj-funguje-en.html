AI Marketing Analytics for SMEs: When Data Creates Value

Author: Martina Mundová

Artificial intelligence promises better customer insights, more precise campaign targeting and faster analysis. A small business, however, may lack reliable data and specialist staff. A study published in the Journal of Marketing Analytics in October 2026 therefore looks beyond the availability of tools. It examines the conditions under which small and medium-sized enterprises can actually use AI for marketing decisions.

What the study investigated

The authors explored the intersection of AI applications in marketing analytics and organizational readiness among SMEs. Marketing analytics is not merely generating advertisements or social posts. It also involves understanding customer segments, evaluating campaigns and supporting decisions about which actions create measurable value. Technology can help only when the firm knows which business question the analysis should address.

Evidence and method

The study brings together a systematic review of 49 scientific publications and a questionnaire survey of more than 100 SMEs. The researchers compared the possibilities described in academic literature with how businesses perceive and use AI in practice. This combined design helps reveal the gap between what technology might do under ideal conditions and what a small organization can realistically implement.

Four forms of AI use

The proposed framework distinguishes opportunistic use, operational use, strategic readiness and strategic AI use. These are conceptual categories, not grades or a compulsory sequence for every company. They help separate occasional use of a text generator from an organization that systematically employs verified information to support customer segmentation, marketing investment and other strategic choices.

Benefits and adoption barriers

The study identifies organizational, technological and user-related conditions shaping adoption. Common practical constraints include limited staff capacity, digital skills and data quality. Potential benefits include better information processing, forecasting and analytical support. None of those possibilities is proof that a particular application, introduced without oversight, will automatically generate more profitable campaigns.

An example for entrepreneurs

Consider an online retailer uncertain about which customer groups buy repeatedly. Before buying an advanced AI platform, the retailer could examine purchase records, marketing consent and methods for calculating campaign returns. Only then might a segmentation or prediction pilot be worth testing. This scenario is an editorial application of the findings about data readiness, not an experiment carried out by the research team.

Limits of the evidence

A literature review combined with a survey of more than 100 firms provides valuable context but not a universal calculation of financial return. The observed relationships are not proof that a particular tool causes increased sales. Handling customer data also requires transparent policies, legal compliance and data minimization. Technical capability must not be confused with permission or a defensible business case.

Conclusion

The key issue for entrepreneurs is whether information can lead to a better decision. The study supports starting with data quality and organizational capability before choosing an AI product. AI becomes a meaningful part of marketing strategy only when it has a defined purpose, manageable risks and results that a company can independently assess.
