# From Feedback to Design: Why User Data Often Fails to Shape Space

Modern corporate real estate and workspace creation increasingly emphasize evidence-based decision-making. However, despite the availability of post-occupancy evaluation tools, their practical utilization remains limited. New research provides valuable insights into how practitioners handle user feedback and where a critical gap emerges in translating data into actual design.

### Introduction

The discussion on how behavioral metrics and user needs influence the design of workplaces is vital for modern business management, architecture, and digital design. While theory assumes that quantitative and qualitative indicators directly shape the final form of spaces or digital products, reality is far more complex. The starting point for reflection is the fact that the mere availability of sophisticated measurement tools does not automatically guarantee their meaningful practical use.

This article offers a critical review of the latest scientific findings published in the Journal of Corporate Real Estate under DOI 10.1108/jcre-06-2026-0035. Authors Maria Victoria Davalos Quevedo, Alessandra Luna-Navarro, Uta Pottgiesser, and Ulrich Blum investigated why validated evaluation tools remain on the periphery of attention. They focused particularly on how designers and consultants actually interpret and translate user information into concrete decisions.

### What the study examined

According to the abstract, the study aims to investigate why validated post-occupancy evaluation (POE) tools remain marginal despite their widespread availability and the growing emphasis on evidence-based workplace decision-making. While previous research identified barriers to POE adoption, less was known about how workplace practitioners collect, interpret, and translate user-related information into workplace design decisions.

The methodology, as stated in the abstract, involved sixteen semi-structured expert interviews conducted with workplace designers, architects, and consultants operating primarily in Germany. Interviews explored project workflows, methods for understanding user needs, questionnaire use, and approaches to translating user feedback into design decisions, with data analyzed through an inductive qualitative coding process.

### Findings and interpretation

According to the abstract, workspace design operates through interpretive and narrative forms of reasoning rather than standardized evaluation. Workshops and conversations dominated user engagement, while questionnaires served communicative rather than analytical purposes. These findings highlight the underlying mechanisms creative professions use when processing unstructured human inputs.

A key contribution of the study is the identification of the "interpretation gap." According to the abstract, this refers to the absence of frameworks that help practitioners translate evaluation outputs into concrete spatial decisions. Consequently, user feedback remains disconnected from design action despite practitioners' genuine interest in evidence, explaining why available data often fails to influence outcomes.

### Methodology and limitations

When evaluating these outcomes, it must be noted that the analysis draws exclusively from the provided abstract excerpt, and the complete full-text article was not verified. The methodological foundation relies on a qualitative sample of sixteen practitioners from Germany, meaning the derived conclusions provide analytical rather than statistical generalization. While valuable for deep insights, this requires caution when applying the findings universally across different geographic or cultural markets.

The authors themselves note in the summary that future research should test these patterns in larger samples and develop methods that better support the translation of user feedback into spatial design decisions. Therefore, the professional public should treat this as a qualitative perspective opening doors to further inquiry rather than a universal quantitative law.

### WEBU practical proposal

Building on the principles discussed in the study, we provide editorial recommendations for areas such as web design, marketing, and small business management, which frequently encounter the same "interpretation gap" when analyzing user data. Small business owners and managers often collect feedback via surveys or reviews, yet lack a system to transform them into actionable changes in service offerings or internal processes.

In web design and marketing, this implies that passively collecting traffic metrics or running one-off satisfaction surveys is insufficient. The editorial team recommends establishing regular interpretation sessions where marketing and development teams jointly review what data reveals about actual customer behavior, translating insights into two or three concrete interface or campaign improvements.

### Conclusion

Examining the link between user feedback and actual design actions reveals a profound challenge in contemporary practice. According to the study's abstract, improving workplace evaluation practice requires more than increasing the availability of measurement tools, with focus shifting toward interpretive usability and connecting outputs to planning.

From the WEBU editorial perspective, data alone does not guarantee success. The key to effective growth in businesses, workspaces, and digital projects is the ability to bridge the gap between people's insights and the courage to turn them into meaningful action.
