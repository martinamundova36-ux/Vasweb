Financial Flexibility in SMEs: Evidence from 15,079 Firms

Author: Martina Mundová

Running a business is not only about reported profit. It is also about whether the company can fund ordinary operations and respond to unforeseen costs. A study published in the Central European Management Journal in August 2026 analyzed the financial flexibility of 15,079 SMEs from nine Central and Eastern European countries. It draws attention to an essential distinction: cash reserves, access to borrowing, working capital and profitability are related, but they are not interchangeable.

What financial flexibility means

A financially flexible firm has room to react to a revenue shortfall or an investment opportunity. This does not necessarily mean holding unusually large amounts of cash. Unused borrowing capacity and access to financing on reasonable terms may also matter. Distinguishing immediately available money, working capital and longer-term financing options helps managers identify weaknesses that an attractive profit figure can hide.

Data and methods

The authors examined financial information from 15,079 businesses in nine countries across Central and Eastern Europe between 2014 and 2022. Their analysis focused on relationships among financial flexibility, profitability and working capital management. This was an observational statistical study of company records, not a randomized intervention giving identical credit facilities to different firms. The findings describe observed relationships rather than guaranteeing the outcome of one action.

Main findings

Within the sample, borrowing capacity was a major source of financial flexibility. More financially flexible SMEs were associated with higher profitability and a larger working-capital share of total assets. However, greater flexibility was not associated with a changed cash conversion cycle. The researchers also report that financial flexibility moderated the negative relationship between working-capital levels and profitability, making that relationship less severe among more flexible firms.

Reading the results carefully

Two firms can record the same sales while having very different ability to handle a late customer payment or finance seasonal inventory. Accounting profit is not the same as cash availability. Equally, holding excessive cash or stock can tie up resources that might otherwise produce a return. A good financial policy balances resilience and efficiency rather than maximizing every financial ratio without considering its opportunity cost.

Questions an owner can ask

How long could ordinary costs be covered after a revenue drop? How much cash is tied up in stock and outstanding invoices? How many days pass between paying suppliers and receiving customers’ money? Is a backup financing option available, and on what terms? These are editorial management questions, not tailored financial advice. Interest costs, repayment terms and the risks of additional debt must be assessed independently.

Limitations

The sample covers selected countries and an earlier time period. Relationships may differ among industries, startups or periods with different borrowing conditions. The authors also acknowledge a relatively limited set of external determinants in their statistical model. Observational associations should not be interpreted as evidence that simply opening a credit line will automatically raise profits.

Conclusion

Financial flexibility is broader than the balance in a current account. For a small company, it reflects the ability to respond without immediately threatening operations. Evidence from Central and Eastern Europe indicates that working-capital decisions should be considered together with financing capacity and profitability. The study offers a more useful set of questions for planning resilient business growth.
