Influence of Mentorship Practices on Employee Retention in State-Owned Health Institutions in Kenya

Author: Martina Mundová

This research digest provides an analysis of a study published in the Academic Journal of Humanities and Social Sciences Research, exploring the relationship between mentorship practices and employee retention within public health institutions in Nairobi. According to the abstract, the research focuses on the public sector and offers statistical data on correlations while raising important questions for managerial practice.

Introduction

Employee retention remains a critical challenge for modern organizations across all sectors of the economy. A shortage of experienced workers and high staff turnover can significantly undermine operational stability and the overall competitiveness of businesses regardless of their specific industry focus. According to the abstract, this study centers on state-owned healthcare institutions in Nairobi City County, Kenya, where management faces distinct pressures regarding workforce stability.

While many organizations invest in various forms of non-financial benefits, mentorship is frequently cited as a key instrument for supporting professional growth. Authors Roselant Boen and Selline Indara Akoyo examined the extent to which formal and informal mentorship practices influence the decision of employees to remain within their organization, drawing conceptually on social exchange theory and organizational support principles.

What the study examined

According to the abstract, the main purpose of this research was to determine the influence of mentorship practices on employee retention within state-owned health institutions located in Nairobi City County. Specifically, the authors tracked how mentorship contributes to overall employee commitment, career development opportunities, and the stated intention of staff to remain in public health institutions, guided by social exchange theory.

The target population comprised a total of 7,358 employees operating across the specified state-run facilities. From this extensive population, 379 respondents were selected through stratified random sampling to provide the necessary primary data. The researchers combined quantitative and qualitative data collection approaches, utilizing structured questionnaires and interviews to gather comprehensive insights from the participants.

The examined variables encompassed perceptions of mentorship practices, expressed through mean scores ranging between 2 85 and 3 15, alongside the identification of major barriers to mentorship implementation. Key challenges monitored included the absence of formal structures, a shortage of senior staff members, and severe time constraints, all tested against measures of organizational commitment.

Findings and interpretation

According to the abstract, the findings revealed mixed perceptions of mentorship practices among employees, with mean scores ranging from 2 85 to 3 15. The major challenges identified by participants included a lack of formal mentorship structures cited by 30 8 percent, inadequate senior staff reported by 25 4 percent, and time constraints affecting 21 7 percent of respondents.

Regarding employee attitudes, 59 5 to 63 6 percent of respondents expressed organizational commitment and a clear intention to remain with the institution. However, concurrently, 47 6 to 49 3 percent of participants reported that they were considering leaving their jobs, highlighting an internal dichotomy and the presence of conflicting motivational factors within the workplace environment.

Statistical analysis via Pearson correlation indicated a strong positive relationship between mentorship and employee retention, with an r value of 0 671 at p less than 0 01. Subsequent regression analysis established a statistically significant positive influence of mentorship practices on employee retention, yielding a beta coefficient of 0 231 at p less than 0 001, highlighting a modest yet tangible contribution to workforce stability.

Methodology and limitations

According to the abstract, the study adopted a descriptive cross-sectional research design that successfully integrated quantitative and qualitative approaches. Quantitative data were analyzed utilizing descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, and regression analysis, while qualitative data were evaluated through thematic analysis, thereby offering a broader perspective on the investigated phenomenon.

A primary limitation of this research is that the available source text is solely a secondary summary in the form of an abstract. This means the complete research article, full survey questions, precise sample breakdowns, and detailed qualitative transcripts were not independently verified through full-text reading. Furthermore, observed correlations do not automatically imply direct causation, and unmeasured confounding variables may exist.

Additionally, the research was conducted within the specific context of state-owned healthcare institutions in Kenya, which severely limits the direct generalization of findings to other sectors such as private enterprises, technology start-ups, or manufacturing firms operating under different economic conditions. Entrepreneurs must carefully consider these contextual boundaries when interpreting insights for their own ventures.

Business relevance and limitations

According to the abstract, the authors recommend institutionalizing structured mentorship programs, ensuring equitable access, allocating dedicated mentoring time, and strengthening career development opportunities. Entrepreneurs in ordinary commercial settings, however, must remain cautious against directly transferring these findings, as the public sector in a developing nation operates under significantly different conditions than standard market-driven businesses.

For example, in a mid-sized software firm, management could test the implementation of a formal six-month mentorship program for junior developers while tracking turnover rates and job satisfaction via regular anonymous surveys. This setup would allow the organization to evaluate whether investing in senior mentors actually reduces departures, while accounting for potential confounding factors like salary adjustments or other employee benefits.

It is essential to recognize that the correlation established in Kenyan healthcare does not guarantee identical economic returns in a distinct commercial environment. Entrepreneurs should approach these results critically, validate data within their own teams, and avoid relying solely on the universal efficacy of mentorship without continuously measuring its actual impact on employee turnover.

Conclusion

The study offers valuable insights into the relationship between mentorship practices and staff stability within the demanding environment of public healthcare institutions. According to the abstract, the results confirm a statistically significant yet modest influence of mentorship on employee retention, underscoring its relevance as a component of broader organizational support frameworks.

At the same time, the findings demonstrate that mentorship alone cannot resolve overarching turnover challenges if institutions lack formal structures, adequate senior personnel, and sufficient time allocations. For managers and entrepreneurs, this implies that mentorship should be integrated alongside recognition systems, transparent promotion procedures, and supportive working conditions to achieve sustainable workforce retention.
