Socially Responsible Leadership and Employee Green Behavior

Author: Martina Mundová

This study examines how socially responsible leadership influences employee green behavior within manufacturing firms in Ghana. The research highlights the role of affective organizational commitment as a vital psychological mechanism connecting leadership styles with environmental actions. For entrepreneurs, it offers insights into how management values and emotional employee engagement can foster more sustainable workplace practices in emerging economies.

Introduction

In the current global business environment, there is an increasing emphasis on corporate environmental responsibility. This study focuses on the concept of socially responsible leadership (SRL) and its impact on employee green behavior (EGB). Researchers analyze whether leaders who demonstrate social responsibility can more effectively motivate their subordinates toward environmentally oriented activities by strengthening their emotional attachment to the organization.

The research specifically targets the context of an emerging economy, namely the Ghanaian manufacturing sector. According to the abstract, the study examines whether affective organizational commitment (AOC) acts as a mediator of this relationship. This approach allows for a better understanding of the psychological processes behind employee environmental behavior and offers a theoretical framework for managers who want to integrate sustainability principles into their daily corporate culture and human resource management.

What the study examined

The study focused on identifying the relationship between socially responsible leadership and employee green behavior. According to the abstract, the authors examined whether the perception of leaders' social responsibility leads to higher levels of green behavior among employees. A key variable was affective commitment, defined as an employee's emotional attachment, identification, and involvement with the organization, which is distinct from commitment based on perceived costs or felt obligation.

The research objective centered on 15 manufacturing firms operating in five sub-sectors: Steel and Metal Production, Food and Beverage Processing, Textiles and Apparel Manufacturing, Petrochemicals and Plastics, and Pharmaceuticals. The goal was to determine if an employee's emotional bond to the firm acts as a mechanism through which responsible leadership manifests in specific green actions at work, while avoiding a simplified understanding of organizational commitment.

Findings and interpretation

According to the abstract, the results indicate that socially responsible leadership is positively associated with employee green behavior and affective commitment. The study found that affective commitment partially mediates this relationship. This suggests that employees who feel a strong emotional bond to the organization due to their leaders' responsible approach tend to exhibit green behavior more frequently. This mechanism suggests that emotional engagement is an important channel for environmental initiatives.

The significance of these findings lies in confirming that environmental behavior is not merely the result of formal policies but also of employees' emotional identification with company values. According to the abstract, the study does not claim a fundamentally new theoretical integration but offers a valuable refinement of responsible leadership mechanisms. For managers, this means that building emotional bonds through responsible leadership can be an effective strategy for supporting environmental goals within the manufacturing sector.

Methodology and limitations

The research utilized a cross-sectional survey design, with data collected from employees across 15 manufacturing firms. Structural equation modeling (SEM), supplemented with bootstrapping procedures, was used to test the hypothesized relationships. According to the abstract, these results must be viewed with caution, as the cross-sectional nature of the study does not allow for definitive causal inferences regarding the relationship between the variables.

Another limitation, according to the abstract, is the reliance on self-reported measures, which may raise concerns regarding common method variance. The authors recommend that future research utilize longitudinal or time-lagged designs to enhance causal robustness. It is also necessary to consider whether these findings from the Ghanaian manufacturing sector are fully generalizable to other industrial contexts or geographic regions, which remains a subject for further investigation.

Business relevance and limitations

Entrepreneurs can learn from this research that investing in socially responsible leadership is not just an ethical choice but also a tool to support employee green behavior. If leaders communicate and live by values of responsibility, they can strengthen employees' emotional bond to the firm, which in turn supports environmental initiatives. However, it is important to remember that these results come from the Ghanaian industrial environment and may not apply universally.

For example, an entrepreneur in a Central European manufacturing plant could implement programs where leaders actively involve employees in decision-making regarding energy savings. Subsequently, it would be appropriate to measure whether the rate of employee initiatives in recycling or resource conservation has increased. However, it is necessary to distinguish this emotional engagement from other forms of commitment, such as contractual obligation, and observe whether behavioral changes are truly related to the perception of the leader.

Conclusion

The study provides a valuable perspective on the link between socially responsible leadership and employee green behavior through affective commitment. According to the abstract, it was confirmed that emotional attachment to the organization is a key factor mediating the influence of leaders on environmental activities. Although the study presents interesting findings, its limitations in terms of cross-sectional design and the specific context of the Ghanaian industry require caution when interpreting and applying results in other settings.

For managers, the key takeaway remains that supporting environmental behavior requires more than just technical measures; it requires building an emotional relationship with employees. Future studies will need to further explore how these mechanisms manifest in different cultural and industrial conditions. For now, it is appropriate to view these results as a prompt to reflect on how leadership style influences not only performance but also the environmental footprint of an organization.
