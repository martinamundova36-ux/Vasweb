Implementing GenAI in Small Businesses: A Practical Perspective

Author: Martina Mundová

This research digest analyzes practical recommendations for small businesses integrating generative AI (GenAI). Based on the abstract of a study published in the Journal of Social Impact in Business Research, we focus on three key pillars: treating AI as a team member, prioritizing customer needs, and conducting small-scale trials to ensure effective implementation within real-world business environments.

Introduction

The rapid rise of generative artificial intelligence presents both opportunities and significant uncertainty for small and medium-sized enterprises. According to the abstract of the study by Greg Harford (2026), there is a pressing need to bridge the gap between academic research and the daily practices of small firms. This article synthesizes practical insights intended to help entrepreneurs integrate new technologies effectively while mitigating unnecessary operational risks.

The study authors aim to provide a voice for small businesses within the academic discourse on technology. According to the abstract, the goal of this commentary is to translate theoretical findings from the journal's special issue into actionable steps. For entrepreneurs, understanding that successful AI adoption is not merely a technological challenge, but primarily a matter of organizational culture and strategic planning with limited resources, is essential.

What the study examined

The study focused on providing a practitioner's perspective for small businesses within the context of broader research on the impacts of GenAI. According to the abstract, the author analyzed articles from a special issue of the journal to extract applicable insights. The research question centered on how small firms can effectively implement GenAI while avoiding common pitfalls associated with focusing excessively on the tool itself rather than solving actual business problems.

Methodologically, the study employs a conceptual approach drawing on practice-informed insights. According to the abstract, the study identifies three main pillars of implementation: treating AI as a new team member, prioritizing customer value over technological enthusiasm, and building capability through small, controlled trials. The scope of the study is limited to the small business perspective and does not claim universal applicability to all organizational types without further verification.

Findings and interpretation

According to the abstract, the study presents three fundamental findings. The first is the necessity of treating GenAI as a new team member, suggesting a need for integration into existing processes and team dynamics. The second finding is the recommendation to start with the customer, not the tool; technology should serve to solve specific client pain points. The third pillar is building capability through small trials, which minimizes operational risks associated with large investments.

The significance of these findings lies in their pragmatism. According to the abstract, this commentary is one of the first in an academic journal to provide a voice for small businesses. The interpretation of these findings suggests that success depends on iterative learning and adaptation. It is important to note, however, that the abstract does not provide quantitative data on the success rates of these strategies, so they should be viewed as strategic recommendations rather than empirically verified causal models.

Methodology and limitations

The study utilizes a conceptual approach based on synthesizing insights from other scholarly articles. According to the abstract, the author drew on practice-informed insights, meaning this is not a primary empirical field study. This approach is suitable for generating theoretical frameworks but limits the study's ability to predict precise outcomes across different industries or geographic contexts. The methodology is therefore qualitative and interpretive rather than statistically testable.

Key limitations include the absence of specific data regarding sample size or the demographics of the businesses to which the recommendations apply. According to the abstract, the study focuses on small businesses but does not specify whether findings differ by industry or level of digitalization. The reader must consider that the abstract provides no statistical tests or control groups, meaning all conclusions are based on expert synthesis and require caution during application.

Business relevance and limitations

Entrepreneurs can use these findings to structure their approach to AI. According to the abstract, it is crucial not to invest in technology without a clear goal. For example, if a company considers introducing AI for customer support, it should first test the tool on a small sample of inquiries (a pilot trial), measuring response time and customer satisfaction, rather than implementing it across the board, which could disrupt existing workflows.

Another important lesson is cultural integration. According to the abstract, AI should be viewed as a colleague, meaning employees should be given space to understand its functions and limitations. The entrepreneur should monitor whether the introduction of AI actually simplifies team work or brings additional administrative burdens. These steps require patience and a willingness to iterate, with success measured by the real value delivered to the customer.

Conclusion

Greg Harford's study provides a valuable framework for small businesses facing the challenge of GenAI integration. According to the abstract, the key to success is pragmatism, a customer-centric focus, and gradual adaptation. Although the study does not provide empirical data on causality, its contribution lies in guiding entrepreneurs through the complex environment of new technologies.

For entrepreneurs, the most important lesson remains the need for critical thinking when adopting innovations. According to the abstract, it is necessary to avoid technological determinism and instead seek ways in which AI can genuinely support the business. Future research should supplement these conceptual recommendations with quantitative studies that would confirm the effectiveness of these strategies under various market conditions.
